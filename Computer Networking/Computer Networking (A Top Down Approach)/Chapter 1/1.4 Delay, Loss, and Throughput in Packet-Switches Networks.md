


#### ***1.4.1 Overview of Delay in Packet-Switched Networks***
*A packet starts in a host system (source), passes through a series of routers, and ends its journey in another host (destination). As a packet travels from one node (host or router) to another node the packet suffers from several types of delays at EACH node along the path.*

- ***Nodal processing delay***
- ***Queueing delay***
- ***Transmission delay***
- ***Propagation delay***
Together, the above add up to what's called ***Total Nodal Delay***
$$
d_{nodal} = d_{proc}\;+\;d_{queue}\;+\;d_{trans}\;+\;d_{prop}
$$

---

#### ***Nodal Processing Delay***
- Time required to examine the packet's header and determine where to direct the packet.
- Time needed to check for bit-level errors in the packet that was transmitted from a node.
- Other things...

---

#### ***Queueing Delay***
- Packet waits to be transmitted onto the link.
- Length of the delay depends on number of earlier-arriving packets that are queued.
- If queue is empty, delay is zero.
- If queue is congested, delay will be long.
- Queueing delays can be microseconds to milliseconds.

---

#### ***Transmission Delay***
- Packets are (most commonly) transmitted in a first-come-first-serve manner.
- packet can be transmitted only after all packets that arrived before it have been transmitted.
- Transmission delays can be from microseconds to milliseconds.

---

#### ***Propagation Delay***
- Time taken to propagate a bit from the beginning of the current link to the next node.
- Propagation speed depends on the physical medium of the link (fiber optics, twisted-pair, etc...)
- In WAN networks, propagation delays are on the order of milliseconds

*Propagation Speed is defined as*
$$
2\;\times 10^8\;meters/sec\; to \; 3\;\times 10^8\;meters/sec
$$

*Propagation delay is therefore defined as*
$$
distance\;between\;two\;routers / propagation\;speed
$$


> [!NOTE] 
> 
> Transmission delay is the amount of time required for the router to push out the packet. It is a function of the packet's length and the transmission rate of the link. NOTHING to do with distance between the two routers.
> 
> Propagation delay is the time it takes for a bit to propagate from one router to the next, it is a function of the distance between two routers. 

---

#### ***1.4.2 Queueing Delay and Packet Loss***
*Queueing delay can vary from packet to packet*

- Out of 10 packets, if the first packet arrives at an empty queue, no queuing delay, the last one will suffer large queuing delay.
- Therefore, we measure queuing delay via average queuing delay, variance of queuing delay, and probability that queueing delay exceeds a specified value.

Queueing delay depends on whether traffic arrives periodically or in bursts.

$$
a = avg\;rate\;packets\;arrive\;at\;queue\;(packets/sec)
$$
$$
R = transmission\;rate\;(bits/sec)
$$
$$
L_a=avg\;rate\;bits\;arrive\;at\;queue
$$
Thus
$$
L_a/R = traffic\;intensity
$$
If traffic intensity is > 1 then average rate bits arrive at the queue exceeds the transmission rate of bits out of the queue.

Queue will increase without bound and approach infinity.

#### ***1.4.3 End-To-End Delay***
*Nodal delay is the delay at a single router. End-To-End delay is the total delay from source to destination*

Suppose there are N-1 Routers between the source host and destination host.
Lets also suppose that the network is uncongested (queueing delays negligible)

Processing delay at each router and at source host is
$$
d_{proc}
$$
Transmission rate out of each router and out of source host is 
$$
R_{Bits/sec}
$$
and propagation delay is 
$$
d_{prop}
$$
Nodal delays accumulate and thus the end-to-end delay is
$$
d_{end-to-end} = N(d_{proc}+d_{trans}+d_{prop})
$$
Also remember that 
$$
d_{trans} = L/R
$$
Where L is the packet size in bits.
