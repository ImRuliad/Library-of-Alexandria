## ***3.6.1 The Causes and the Costs of Congestion***
---

### Scenario 1: Two Senders, a Router with Infinite Buffers
Imagine a scenario with two hosts (A and B) each have a congestion that shares a single hop between source and destination.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-30 at 8.08.00 PM.png]]

- Host A is sending data into the connection at an avg rate of $b_{in} \ bytes/sec$ 
- Data is original (Each unit of data only sent once into the socket)
- Data encapsulated and sent (no error recovery, retransmission, flow control, or congestion control)
- No transport and lower-layer header info
- Thus, the rate that Host A sends traffic is $b_{in} \ bytes/sec$
- Host B operates in the same manner as Host A
- Host A and B share pass through a router over a shared outgoing link of capacity $R$
- Router has buffers that store incoming packets when packet-arrival rate exceeds outgoing link's capacity
- Assume router has infinite amount of buffer space

![[Screenshot 2026-10-30 at 8.14.31 PM.png]]
This figure plots the performance of Host A's connection under the first scenario. The left graph plots the
per-connection throughput (bytes/sec at the receiver) as a function of the connection-sending rate.

- For a sending rate between 0 and $R/2$ the throughput at the receiver equals the sender's sending rate
- Everything sent by the sender is received at the receiver with a finite delay
- When the sending rate is above $R/2$ the throughput is only $R/2$
- This upper limit is the consequence of sharing the link capacity between two connections.
- No matter how high Hosts A and B set their sending rates, they will never see a throughput higher than $R/2$

## 3.6.2 Approaches to Congestion Control
---

### End-to-End Congestion Control
*In end-to-end congestion control, the network layer provides no explicit support to the transport layer for congestion control*

- The presence of network congestion must be inferred by the end systems based only on observed network behavior
- TCP takes this end-to-end approach towards congestion control (IP layer is not required to provide feedback to hosts regarding congestion control)
- TCP segment loss (indicated by timeout or three duplicate ACKs) is taken as indication of network congestion
- TCP decreases its window size accordingly
- A more recent proposal for TCP congestion control uses increasing its round-trip segment delay as an indicator of network congestion

### Network-Assisted Congestion Control
*Routers provide explicit feedback to the sender and receiver regarding the congestion state of the network*

- May be as simple as a single bit indicating congestion at a link
- More sophisticated feedback exists like ATM Available Bite Rate (ABR) congestion control
- with ABR, a router informs the sender of the maximum host sending rate (the router) can support on an outgoing link
- Internet-default versions of IP and TCP adopt an end-to-end approach

Congestion information is typically fed back from the network to the sender in one of two ways
- Direct feedback, a choke packet, essentially indicates a congested network
- Router marks or updates a field in a packet flowing from sender to receiver to indicate congestion.
	- Upon receipt of the marked packet, receiver notifies sender of congestion indication.
	- Takes a full RTT
